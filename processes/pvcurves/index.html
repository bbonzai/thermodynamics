Isochoric Pressurization: The work done is 0, since the volume does not change. But pressure increases; since \(P \propto T\), the gas heats up. The warming gas increases the internal energy \(\Delta U = \frac{5}{2} N k \Delta T\), for a diatomic gas like ordinary air, \(k\) is Boltzmann's constant, and \(N\) is the number of molecules in the gas.

Isobaric Expansion: Work is done by the expanding system, since \(W = P\Delta V\). The expansion happens because heat is supplied to the system ($Q > 0). The internal energy of the system is difference between the heat supplied to and the work done by the system.

Adiabatic Expansion: During adiabatic expansion, no heat is transferred to the system, \(Q = 0\).
But the volume increases, meaning that the system does work \(W\) on the outside world. Since \(W > 0\), that work comes at the expense of internal energy: \(\Delta U = -W\).

Isochoric Decompression: The system does no work since the volume does not change. But pressure drops because \(P \propto T\) and \(T\) is dropping. The temperature drop produces an equal drop in internal energy \(\Delta U\).

Isobaric Collapse: \(P = {\text constant}\), and \(V\) decreases. The decrease in \(V\) happens because the outside world is doing work on the gas. It does this by extracting heat from the system: (\(V_{\text final}/\)V_{initial} \propto \(T_{\text final}/T_{\text initial}\)).
The temperature drop decreases the internal energy because \(\Delta U\) is proportional to \(T\) for an ideal gas. The heat transfer into the system is negative ($Q = \Delta U + W) because both \(W\) and \(\Delta U\) are negative.
