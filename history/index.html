Thermodynamics History

\(\sim1700\): The development of the first steam engines.

This event was the driving force behind the development of thermodynamic theory. The first engines were very inefficient.

1738: Daniel Bernoulli presented the kinetic theory of gases

1742: Celsius defined \(0^\circ\) as the boiling point of water and \(100^\circ\) C as its freezing point.

But it wasn't until 1778 that the biologist, Carl Linnaeus (a botanist and physician), set the temperature scale "right", making defined \(0^\circ\)C as the freezing point of water and \(100\degree\) C as its boiling point.

1. Joseph Black (1728-1799) (Scottish) (University of Glasgow)

Known for:

  • 1750: invention of the analytical balance, which was far more accurate than other balances at that time.
  • 1754: the discovery of carbon dioxide. Black called it "fixed air" bc it was fixed inside solids. He discovered it by heating limestone.
  • 1755: the discovery of Magnesium. Black distinguished between magnesia (now called magnesium oxide) and lime (now called calcium oxide).
  • 1760: discovery of specific heat
  • 1761: discovery of latent heat

1765: [James Watt] developed the first practical steam engine.

1778: [James Watt] showed that heat is not completely convertible to work.

1782: Lavoisier and Laplace

Calulated the heat involved in various chemical changes using Black's discovery of latent heat.

1798: [Count Rumford, aka Benjamin Thomson] challenged the caloric theory

The caloric theory claimed that heat was an indestructible fluid.

1801: John Dalton presented the kinetic theory of gases.

Showed that the pressure exerted by a mixture of gases was the sum of the pressure exerted by individual components. (Temperature is a meaure of particles' speed (gas) or vibration (solid).

[Was this 1801 or 1808?]

2. Nicolas Leonard Sadi Carnot (French) (1796-1832)

At the age of 30, Sadi Carnot published a book, "Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire", in which he defined a number of thermodynamic relationships, some of which were not recognized until years after his death.

Known for:

  • 1824: Carnot cycle
  • 1824: Carnot heat engine
  • 1824: Thermal efficiency
  • 1824: Carnot theorem
  • 1824: Second law of thermodynamics: Heat always flows from hotter to colder regions
  • 1824: Clausius–Clapeyron relation

1842: Mayer showed that energy cannot be created or destroyed

This is the law of conservation of energy.

3. James Prescott Joule (English Physicist) (1818-1889)

He is called "the first thermodynamic experimentalist", and is best known for discovering the first law of thermodynamics, that

\[ \Delta U = Q - W \]

where * \(Q\) is heat added to the system, and * \(W\) is work done by the system

  • 1843 First law of thermodynamics $\Delta U = \(\Delta Q - \Delat 1\)
  • 1850: Disproving caloric theory
  • Joule expansion
  • Joule heating
  • Joule–Thomson effect
  • Gough–Joule effect
  • Magnetostriction
  • Mechanical equivalent of heat

4. Rudolf Clausius (1822-1888), German

1850: discovered the property of entropy (\(S\)), and that changes in entropy (during a reversible process) are related to heat exchanged between system and surroundings by \(\Delta S = Q/T\).

1850: heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder body to a warmer body.

  • Chemical thermodynamics
  • 1850: First law of thermodynamics
  • 1850, 1856: Second law of thermodynamics
  • 1865: Originator of the concept of entropy
  • Entropy production
  • Disgregation
  • Ideal gas law
  • Kinetic theory of gases
  • Virial theorem
  • Clausius model
  • Clausius theorem
  • Clausius–Mossotti relation
  • Clausius–Clapeyron relation
  • Clausius–Duhem inequality

5. Lord Kelvin, aka William Thomson (1824-1907) (University of Glasgow)

  • Introduced the notion of absolute zero temperature

Born in Ireland, but did his research in Scotland and University of Glasgow.
His grandfather was Scotch, and his father eventually appointed as a professor of mathematics at University of Glasgow.

Known for:

1848: (most important): Landmark paper, where he proposed there was a temperature below which nothing could exist. This is "absolute zero", \(273^{\circ}\) below \(0{^\circ}\) on the Celsius scale.

1851: Published a new concept: "entropy"; heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder body to a hotter body. This is another instantiation of "The Second Law of Thermodynamics".
